Zeta Prime Music · The sound of the primes

Nobody wrote this music. It was hiding in the prime numbers.

2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13… The primes look random: no simple rule tells you where the next one falls. But in 1859 Bernhard Riemann found that they are governed by a hidden set of numbers, the zeros of the zeta function, and that each zero is a wave.

Play those waves together and something uncanny happens: wrong notes drift and wobble, then lock onto the primes, one by one, until the whole number line is in tune.

The mystery

A song inside the primes

The most famous unsolved problem in mathematics

For 167 years, mathematicians have asked whether all of Riemann's zeros lie on a single line. Riemann himself only wrote that it was “very probably” so. This is the Riemann Hypothesis, with a million-dollar prize attached.

The zeros are music

Each zero is a pure tone. One zero alone is just a hum. Add a few and a melody starts to wobble into shape. Add hundreds and the notes land exactly on the primes. Mathematicians call this the explicit formula. Here you can hear it.

This is what it sounds like

Piano, vibraphone, bass and drums, playing only what the zeros dictate. When the band locks in, that is 500 zeros agreeing on where the primes are.

This month a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis was announced: Levent Alpöge released a claimed proof, with a formal version checked in Lean (overview), and mathematicians around the world are now examining it. The zeros you hear have long been computed to lie on Riemann's line. If the proof holds, a 167-year-old mystery is solved: every one of the infinitely many zeros lies on it too.

How it works

From zeros to notes in four steps

Riemann's explicit formula writes the primes as a sum of waves, one per zero. The piece makes each part of that formula audible.

One zero, one wave

The zero ρ = ½ + 14.1347i contributes cos(14.13 ln x): a wave that stretches as x grows. It becomes the lowest drone tone.

Waves add up

With a handful of zeros the sum wobbles, with broad bumps between the integers. These play as out-of-tune ghost notes.

Spikes at the primes

With 200 zeros the waves cancel everywhere except at 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 … and, smaller, at 4, 8, 9, 16, 25, 27.

Spikes become music

Each prime is plucked at the n-th harmonic of G: 3 is a fifth, 5 a major third, 7 a harmonic seventh. Prime powers ring as bells.

A tuning process

“Tuning in” plays the same bar of the number line eight times, each time with more zeros: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 34, 200. You hear the mathematics converge as wrong notes find the primes. At 500 zeros the band locks in.

Arithmetic dynamics

Loudness follows the height of each spike: primes strongest, squares softer, cubes softer still, and prime powers ring on the vibraphone instead of the piano. The accent pattern is the arithmetic of the numbers.

Two pitch worlds

A drone of zeros rooted on E under primes in G: E minor and G major at once. The track plays in equal temperament; the original concept pieces keep the exact, microtonal tuning.

Open source

Render it yourself

A tested Python engine (numpy, scipy, mpmath) with 500 bundled zeros. Scores are TOML files: change the bar length, the key, the number of zeros, and render your own version. MIDI export keeps every microtonal bend.
